Large language models (LLMs) increasingly score student essays at scale,
yet models trained predominantly on Western text risk treating
Anglo-American rhetorical conventions (direct thesis statements, linear
argumentation, assertive hedging) as the universal standard of quality,
penalising structurally valid writing from other cultural traditions.
This thesis formalises this mechanism as \emph{evaluative epistemic
filtering} and develops CAMPS (Culturally-Aware Multi-Perspective
Scoring): an inference-time framework in which multiple evaluator
agents, each calibrated to a distinct cultural interpretation of the
scoring rubric, assess each essay independently before consensus
aggregation. A Bias Divergence Score (BDS) quantifies inter-agent
disagreement and routes culturally contested essays to human review.
A Part~A pilot reported statistically significant scoring bias against
Southeast Asian rhetorical patterns in two frontier models, partial
mitigation under a three-agent panel, and high BDS sensitivity. Part~B
scales this evidence: approximately 200 topic-controlled essays from
the ICNALE corpus (Southeast Asian learners and a native-English
cohort), four frontier and open-weight models, a five-agent panel with
a meta-adjudicator, and validation against multi-rater human ratings.
